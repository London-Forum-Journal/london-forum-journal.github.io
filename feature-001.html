The Architecture of
Transition Intelligence

The London Forum Journal meets AI Systems Architect and independent researcher Anthea Tenayiah Leia Govender to discuss ENNORY, the intelligence of systems in motion, and why the next frontier of computing may not be another answer — but a better understanding of what happens between them.
LONDON FORUM JOURNAL · 31 AUGUST 2026
Technology has a habit of making yesterday's impossible look strangely obvious. Artificial intelligence is now moving from laboratories into hospitals, infrastructure, finance, research and everyday life. Systems are becoming increasingly capable of predicting, generating, classifying and responding. But as the technology becomes more sophisticated, another question begins to emerge: What happens while the system is changing? The London Forum Journal follows emerging technology precisely because the most interesting ideas are not always the ones already sitting comfortably inside established categories. Sometimes the interesting territory is the gap between them. For this issue, we spoke with Anthea Tenayiah Leia Govender, an AI Systems Architect, independent researcher and founder of the ENNORY Intelligence Platform, whose work explores continuity, transition states, geometry and the behaviour of systems while they move between conditions. The conversation is less about predicting the future than examining whether we have been describing intelligent systems correctly in the first place.

The question behind the question

LONDON FORUM JOURNAL
You've worked across artificial intelligence, healthcare innovation and emerging technology. What made you become interested in transition itself?
ANTHEA TENAYIAH LEIA GOVENDER
I became interested in the part that tends to disappear from the description. We often describe systems by their conditions. We measure where something begins and where it ends. But between those conditions, something is happening. A system is changing configuration. Relationships are changing. Information is being carried forward. Some structures are being preserved while others are being altered. That transition is not necessarily empty space. It can contain information. And once you start looking at it that way, you begin asking whether our computational models are giving that territory enough attention.
“The interesting question isn't simply where a system is. It is how the system remains coherent while it is becoming something else.”

Beyond the answer

LONDON FORUM JOURNAL
Is that what you mean by transition intelligence?
ANTHEA TENAYIAH LEIA GOVENDER
Partly.
Partly. We normally associate intelligence with the ability to process information, recognise patterns, make predictions or produce an answer. But I think there is another dimension worth investigating. Can an intelligent system understand its own continuity? Can it understand what changed, what remained stable, which relationships were altered, and what those changes mean for the next configuration? That is where I began thinking about transition intelligence. I don't see it as replacing existing forms of artificial intelligence. I see it as asking whether there is another layer that we have not formalised adequately yet.
LONDON FORUM JOURNAL
That sounds deceptively simple.
ANTHEA TENAYIAH LEIA GOVENDER
The simple questions are often the difficult ones. We can say, “the system changed.” But that doesn't necessarily tell us how it changed. It doesn't necessarily tell us what relationships were maintained. It doesn't necessarily tell us whether the transition was coherent. And it certainly doesn't automatically give us a computational representation of the journey itself.

Why geometry enters the conversation

LONDON FORUM JOURNAL
Your work also introduces geometry. Why?
ANTHEA TENAYIAH LEIA GOVENDER
Because geometry is fundamentally about relationships. A point tells you where something is. A structure tells you how things relate. When you begin thinking about connectivity, direction, adjacency and permitted movement, geometry becomes much more interesting than a picture on a screen. It can become a language for describing how a system is organised and how it moves. That's important to ENNORY. The objective isn't simply to draw a nine-node structure and call it intelligent. The deeper question is whether a constrained geometric structure can provide a useful computational representation of transition.

The ENNORY question

LONDON FORUM JOURNAL
Let's talk about ENNORY specifically. What are you trying to build?
ANTHEA TENAYIAH LEIA GOVENDER
ENNORY is my attempt to give this idea an architecture. At the centre is a nine-node geometric system in which movement is treated as a structured transition rather than simply a sequence of unrelated states. The important distinction is that the nodes aren't intended to be a hierarchy. They represent positions within a constrained relational system. The system can move, redistribute relationships and change configuration while remaining within its defined geometry. That gives me a way to investigate continuity computationally.
LONDON FORUM JOURNAL
And where does this ultimately lead?
ANTHEA TENAYIAH LEIA GOVENDER
Potentially much further than the original question. If you can represent transition, you can start asking whether systems can reason about transition. That could have implications for artificial intelligence, simulation, robotics, complex systems and potentially any environment where understanding change is as important as understanding state. But I'm careful about making that leap too quickly. The architecture has to earn the claim.

The uncomfortable question

LONDON FORUM JOURNAL
Let's challenge it. What if the idea doesn't work?
ANTHEA TENAYIAH LEIA GOVENDER
Then the experiments should tell us. I think that's essential. An interesting idea isn't automatically a scientific result. If ENNORY is going to become meaningful beyond its conceptual foundation, it needs formal definitions, mathematical treatment, implementations and experiments that can test where the framework succeeds and where it doesn't. I'm much more interested in making the proposition testable than making it untouchable.
“An idea becomes interesting to me when you can make it precise enough for someone else to challenge.”

From healthcare to intelligent systems

LONDON FORUM JOURNAL
Your public work has also explored AI, genomics, healthcare innovation and the role of technology in clinical environments. Is there a connection?
ANTHEA TENAYIAH LEIA GOVENDER
Absolutely. Healthcare made the problem of continuity particularly visible to me. A clinical environment isn't really a collection of isolated data points. It's a journey. Something changes. A test produces information. A clinician responds. A treatment changes the patient's condition. Another decision follows. The meaning isn't always contained in one individual data point. It emerges from the relationship between them. That is a very different way of thinking about intelligence. Technology shouldn't simply give us more information. It should help us understand what is changing and why that change matters.

The future isn't standing still

LONDON FORUM JOURNAL
The technology industry is moving incredibly quickly. Where do you think we're going wrong?
ANTHEA TENAYIAH LEIA GOVENDER
I don't think we're necessarily going wrong. I think we're becoming capable of building systems faster than we are developing the conceptual language needed to describe them. AI is changing. Infrastructure is changing. Healthcare is changing. The relationship between humans and computational systems is changing. We therefore need architectures that can deal with change itself. That's the part I'm interested in.

What comes next?

LONDON FORUM JOURNAL
If you could leave researchers and technologists with one question, what would it be?
ANTHEA TENAYIAH LEIA GOVENDER
I'd ask them to look at the part of the system they normally leave unnamed. The transition. The interval. The relationship. The moment where one configuration becomes another. We have become extraordinarily good at describing states. Perhaps the next challenge is learning how to describe the intelligence of moving between them.

The Journal's take

There is something revealing about Govender's proposition precisely because it does not begin with a claim that artificial intelligence has reached its destination. It begins with movement. The London Forum Journal's interest in emerging technology has always been less about predicting which idea will win and more about identifying the questions that technology is beginning to make unavoidable. Transition may be one of them. Whether ENNORY ultimately develops into a useful computational architecture, a broader research framework or something that evolves considerably beyond its present form will depend on formalisation and evidence. But the question behind it is difficult to dismiss: What if the space between states is not merely where computation happens — but something computation should understand? That may be a question worth carrying forward.
ABOUT THE INTERVIEWEE

Anthea Tenayiah Leia Govender is an AI Systems Architect, independent researcher and founder of the ENNORY Intelligence Platform. Her work explores emerging approaches to artificial intelligence, computational systems, transition-state modelling and continuity. Her broader innovation work has also examined AI-enabled healthcare, genomics, smart hospital systems and responsible technology design.
