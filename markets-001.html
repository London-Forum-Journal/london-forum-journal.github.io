When War Reaches the Market

The conflict surrounding Iran is testing more than military alliances. It is testing the economic connections on which the Gulf — and the wider global economy — has come to depend.
LONDON FORUM JOURNAL · SEPTEMBER 2026
Wars are fought on territory. Markets experience them differently. A missile does not need to hit a stock exchange to move it. A tanker does not need to stop completely for shipping costs to rise. An airport does not need to close permanently for airlines, investors and travellers to reconsider their plans. In a connected economy, uncertainty travels through networks. And few places illustrate that reality more clearly than the United Arab Emirates. For decades, Dubai and Abu Dhabi have positioned themselves as connectors — between East and West, capital and opportunity, producers and consumers, passengers and destinations. The current conflict involving Iran is therefore not simply a regional security story for the UAE. It is also an economic stress test.

The first signal comes from the market

Financial markets tend to react before the full economic consequences of a crisis become visible. On August 31, renewed U.S.–Iran tensions pushed Gulf markets lower. Dubai's main share index declined 0.8%, while Abu Dhabi's index fell 0.4%. Oil prices also moved higher as investors reassessed the risks surrounding energy supplies and the Strait of Hormuz. These movements do not mean that the UAE economy has suddenly stopped functioning. They mean something more subtle. Investors are repricing uncertainty. That distinction matters. Markets are not simply asking what is happening today. They are asking what today's events could mean for tomorrow's earnings, trade flows, energy costs, tourism, property demand and capital.

The UAE's unusual position

The UAE occupies an unusual position in the global economy. It is simultaneously an energy producer, a financial centre, a logistics hub, an aviation centre, a tourism destination and a major gateway between markets. That diversification has been one of its greatest economic strengths. But diversification also creates a particular kind of exposure. A country built around connectivity depends on connectivity remaining reliable. Ports need ships. Airports need passengers. Hotels need visitors. Financial centres need confidence. Trade hubs need predictable routes. Real estate depends partly on the confidence of companies and individuals willing to locate themselves there. When geopolitical risk rises, none of these systems necessarily has to fail for economic consequences to appear. A small change in confidence can be enough.
The UAE's economic strength is its connectivity. The question is whether connectivity can remain an advantage when the region around it becomes less predictable.

The Strait that became a market variable

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow maritime passage with an enormous economic significance. Oil and gas moving through the Gulf do not remain a Gulf story once they enter the international market. They become fuel for factories. They become transport costs. They become aviation costs. They become inputs into manufacturing and agriculture. And eventually, some portion of those costs can reach consumers. The International Energy Agency has described the disruption around the Strait as one of the largest oil-market shocks in decades. Its analysis found that alternative routes and additional production could absorb only part of the lost flows, while the UAE's Fujairah facilities outside the Strait became particularly important as an alternative route for energy exports. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3} That creates an important economic distinction. The physical geography may be regional. The price mechanism is global.

The cost of uncertainty

One of the most underestimated economic costs of conflict is uncertainty. A company does not necessarily need to experience a direct loss before changing its behaviour. It may delay an investment. Increase its insurance coverage. Reroute cargo. Hold more inventory. Review employee travel. Change suppliers. Increase security spending. Or simply wait. Each decision can appear insignificant. Collectively, they can change the flow of capital through an economy. This is particularly relevant for a country whose economic model depends heavily on international business. The International Monetary Fund said in July that the UAE economy had demonstrated resilience, supported by strong financial buffers and the rerouting of oil and trade flows. At the same time, it noted that uncertainty related to the conflict had weighed on tourism, transportation, trade and real estate. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4} That is perhaps the clearest picture of the UAE's position. Resilient does not mean unaffected.

Oil can be both shield and shock

Higher oil prices create an unusual dynamic for Gulf economies. For energy exporters, higher prices can support government revenues and external balances. For energy-importing economies, the same price increase can become an immediate cost. The UAE therefore occupies a different position from many countries watching the same price chart. Yet higher prices do not eliminate every risk. If elevated energy costs begin feeding into transportation, manufacturing and food prices globally, central banks may face renewed inflationary pressure. The result is a chain that can extend far beyond the Gulf. A geopolitical event becomes an energy event. The energy event becomes an inflation event. The inflation event becomes a monetary-policy event. And the monetary-policy response can influence investment everywhere.

The shipping equation

Shipping is where geography becomes economics. When maritime risk increases, a vessel's journey is no longer calculated simply in kilometres. It is calculated in insurance. Security. Fuel. Time. Availability. Alternative routes. And the probability of disruption. Recent data has shown that vessel traffic through the Strait has remained well below normal levels during periods of heightened tension. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5} That matters because modern supply chains were designed around efficiency. They assume that goods can move predictably. When predictability disappears, businesses begin paying for resilience. The economic system therefore starts exchanging one principle for another: efficiency for security.

Dubai's relationship with Iran

There is another layer to the UAE story. Dubai has historically maintained significant commercial connections with Iranian businesses and traders. Its position as a regional trading centre made it an important bridge between Iran and international markets. That relationship becomes considerably more complicated when geopolitical pressure intensifies. Recent reporting has described increased pressure on Dubai's long-standing commercial links with Iran, including restrictions and uncertainty surrounding trade and financial activity. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6} This creates a difficult economic balancing act. The UAE has built its prosperity partly by being open to global commerce. But geopolitical conditions can force commercial relationships to be reconsidered. The question becomes not simply: Who do we trade with? but: How do we remain a global trading centre while navigating an increasingly fragmented geopolitical system?

The Gulf is redesigning its infrastructure

One of the most interesting consequences of the conflict may ultimately be visible not in today's market charts, but in tomorrow's infrastructure. Recent reporting has highlighted growing Gulf investment in alternative pipelines, ports and trade infrastructure as governments seek to reduce dependence on vulnerable chokepoints. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7} That is a profound economic response. A crisis changes the calculation. Infrastructure that once appeared unnecessary can suddenly become strategically valuable. An alternative port. A pipeline bypass. A different shipping route. Additional storage. More resilient communications. More diversified suppliers. The economics of redundancy can look wasteful when nothing goes wrong. When something does go wrong, redundancy can become an asset.
The cheapest route is not always the cheapest route when the cost of disruption is included.

The global economy absorbs the shock differently

The world economy has not responded uniformly to the conflict. Some countries are better positioned to absorb higher energy prices. Some are heavily dependent on imports. Some have fiscal space. Others do not. The IMF has warned that the global economy can absorb substantial shocks while still concealing significant differences between countries and regions. Energy importers and economies with limited policy room are particularly exposed. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8} That is why the same barrel of oil can mean very different things in different countries. For an exporter, it can represent revenue. For an airline, it can represent cost. For a manufacturer, it can represent margin pressure. For a household, it can eventually appear in the price of transport and goods. One event. Multiple economies. Different consequences.

What happens if uncertainty becomes normal?

This may be the most important question for investors. Markets can survive shocks. Businesses can adapt. Governments can reroute supplies. Ports can increase capacity. Companies can change suppliers. But adaptation has a price. If geopolitical instability becomes a persistent feature of the operating environment, companies may begin building that uncertainty into investment decisions permanently. That could mean more inventory. More insurance. More alternative suppliers. More regional manufacturing. More strategic infrastructure. More capital held in reserve. In other words, geopolitical risk can gradually become part of the architecture of the economy.

The UAE's resilience test

The UAE entered this period with considerable advantages. Strong financial buffers. A diversified economy. Major infrastructure. International connectivity. Energy resources. A highly developed logistics sector. And an established reputation as a place where global capital can operate. The IMF's assessment in July reflected that resilience, while also acknowledging the effects that uncertainty had already begun to have on parts of the non-hydrocarbon economy. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9} The question for the UAE is therefore not whether it can survive one market shock. It is whether its economic model can continue to thrive if geopolitical uncertainty becomes structurally higher. That is a much larger question.

The Journal's view

The most interesting economic story emerging from the Gulf is not simply the price of oil. It is the price of connection. For decades, the global economy pursued efficiency. Goods moved through the cheapest routes. Capital moved toward the highest returns. Companies built long supply chains. Cities competed to become global hubs. The assumption was that the connections would remain available. The current conflict is challenging that assumption. A port is valuable because ships can reach it. An airport is valuable because people feel confident using it. A financial centre is valuable because capital feels safe moving through it. A trading hub is valuable because counterparties believe tomorrow will look sufficiently like today. Geopolitical instability puts a price on all of those assumptions. And that may be the UAE's most important economic test. Not whether it can withstand a shock. But whether it can remain one of the world's great connectors in a world where the connections themselves are becoming strategic assets.
The next era of globalisation may not be defined by how connected the world can become. It may be defined by how resilient those connections are when they are tested.
